About this project

How I taught my balcony to water itself

A solar-powered irrigation system that understands five different growing zones—and keeps working when the internet does not.

Herbs, flowers, strawberries, and a raised bed all dry out differently. After a spontaneous weekend away during temperatures above 30 °C, I decided the balcony needed a system that could look after every area separately without depending on me being home.

Gravity does the pumping

Simple water, slowly delivered

Three elevated buckets hold around 100 litres. Connected at the bottom, they share one water level and behave like a larger reservoir. Gravity moves the water through individually controlled valves into a Gardena hose system, where drippers deliver it directly to the plants.

There is no pump, which means less power use, less noise, and one fewer failure point. Capacity can change later without redesigning the controller.

Inside the controller

A small computer with a very practical job

An ESP32 reads the moisture sensors, decides whether a zone needs water, pulses its latching valve, updates the local display, and reports measurements. Multiplexers make room for ten sensors and ten control channels without needing a separate pin for each.

Everything lives in a custom enclosure. Numbered, detachable connectors keep the sensors, valves, display, and power wiring serviceable.

The complete system

Local decisions, useful connections

The controller is responsible for the plants. The optional server records telemetry and distributes accepted settings or firmware. The dashboard turns the data into readable history.

If Wi-Fi, the Raspberry Pi, weather service, dashboard, or display disappears temporarily, valid local irrigation decisions continue.

Designed to fail safelyIf one sensor is invalid, the controller can use the other. If both are invalid, the zone is not watered. It reports the problem instead of releasing water using information it cannot trust.

Powered by the sun

Energy only when it is needed

A solar panel charges a LiFePO4 battery. Between evaluations the ESP32 stays in deep sleep, waking to measure, operate a valve, update the display, or report data. Battery state, voltage, temperature, solar current, and power can all reach the dashboard.

A minimum charge level prevents new watering cycles when energy is scarce. Rainwater collection could extend the idea, but on this rented balcony the reservoirs are still filled manually.

A display at a glance

The garden without opening a browser

A three-colour e-paper display shows zone state, time, battery charge and temperature, and current weather. E-paper mainly consumes energy while changing; its last image remains visible while the controller sleeps.

From balcony to browser

A history of what the plants experienced

Regular reports become histories of soil moisture, watering decisions, battery state, solar production, weather, and controller health. The server and dashboard run on a Raspberry Pi, with a Cloudflare Tunnel providing public access.

The trends show how quickly zones dry, whether watering had the expected effect, and how the power system behaves in different conditions.

Made to fit

Hardware shaped around the balcony

The installation combines existing printable designs, adapted parts, and original components. Together they create enclosures, internal carriers, sensor housings, connectors, seals, and parts of the water-distribution system.

The result is a system that can stay installed, be serviced, and adapt as the balcony changes.

Make it yours

Start with one well-tested zone

This gravity-fed solar build is only one arrangement. The controller can be adapted to a regulated water outlet, pumped reservoir, rainwater storage, or fixed low-voltage supply.
